Onboarding guide · for new researchers · September 2026

Verifiable English, from first principles

A research prototype for AI oversight. Write a precise claim in an English-like language. Let an untrusted AI supply a proof. A small checker verifies the deduction; people still review the starting assumptions and whether the formal claim matches the intended question.

Start with sections 1–3; they need no logic background. Then follow the architecture, trust limits and experiments. The final sections explain the vision and how to contribute. Use the glossary as needed. Results and proposals are distinguished throughout.

SECTION 1

The problem: overseeing work you cannot redo yourself

As AI systems get more capable, they will produce work (code, plans, legal or scientific arguments) that a human reviewer cannot fully reproduce. Scalable oversight is the research area asking how a weaker overseer, a person or a weaker model, can still reliably tell good work from bad work produced by a stronger system.

Two families of protocols dominate the literature:

  • Consultancy: one AI argues for an answer, and a judge (human or model) questions it and decides. The AI may be honest or may be arguing for the wrong answer.
  • Debate: two AIs argue opposite sides in front of a judge. The hope is that it is easier to point out a flaw in an argument than to hide one, so the honest side has the advantage.

Debate has a failure mode called obfuscated arguments (Barnes and Christiano, 2020; BC20). A dishonest debater builds a long argument containing a few false sub-claims or invalid steps. The honest debater knows the conclusion is wrong but cannot cheaply find the flaw. Throughout this page, a leaf is a starting claim with no further supporting steps shown. A false leaf and an invalid step are the two kinds of flaw, and the distinction matters for everything that follows: a checker can catch the second kind, never the first.

VE's core idea: factor the decision

Reviewing an argument mixes three questions: Do the steps follow? Are the starting premises true? Does the formal statement say what the author meant? Verifiable English (VE) separates the first question from the other two. Deductions use a small formal logic and are checked mechanically. The residual is what remains for judgement: explicit premises, meaning and omitted requirements. Making these visible does not guarantee that they are few or easy.

Obfuscation: the acceptance rule matters

The project's corrected position (22 September 2026). Under a mandatory-proof rule, nothing is accepted unless the verified checker derives it from declared, reviewed foundations. A dishonest prover then cannot win by assuming false sub-claims. This defeats the Barnes–Christiano construction for fully formalised claims, conditional on those foundations. Earlier project notes said that VE contributes nothing against obfuscated arguments; that was too broad. It holds only for a protocol that accepts leaves without proof.

For a toy example, suppose there are only two boxes, A and B, and the reviewed facts say that box A contains a red token. An arguer claims there are no red tokens, using “box A has none” and “box B has none”. The deduction from those two premises can be valid. Checking that deduction alone misses the false premise. Mandatory proof also requires support for “box A has none”; that obligation cannot pass a sound checker using the reviewed facts.

BC20 itself says that, with enough checking budget, one can just have both debaters write out their entire proof, and run a checker. BC20 takes that checker as given; VE's aim is a checker whose soundness is a proved theorem rather than a capability assumption. BC20, cryptographic example. Prover-estimator debate (PE), section 8, already proposes machine-verifiable mathematical proofs alongside debate for fuzzier claims. PE is a protocol that uses probability estimates for uncertain sub-claims. Combining proofs and debate is not a new VE idea.

  • Soundness is not completion. Soundness means acceptance implies a valid deduction. An honest claim still needs an affordable proof. If it cannot supply one, the result is unresolved, not acceptance of the opposing claim.
  • Proofs can exceed the budget. Full checking does not reach every problem targeted by debate theory, including its PSPACE setting: problems solvable with polynomial memory whose explicit proofs may be too long.
  • Foundations remain assumptions. Physical laws, measurements, idealisations and tolerances may be reviewed but unproved. False or inconsistent foundations can support a bad conclusion.
  • Meaning still needs review. A train modelled as a point particle and one modelled as a rotating rigid body can give different answers. A correct proof of the wrong model does not answer the intended question.
  • Cost matters. One pilot on BC20's iceberg physics example proved its root claim, inside a reviewed physical model, in 912 seconds, using about 2.1 million input tokens, mostly cached, with four failed Lean attempts out of six. It was reviewed only by its author. This is feasibility evidence for one model, not a cost or reliability benchmark.

The benefit comes from sound proof checking, not from English wording. VE also exposes unstated premises and pins definitions across sub-claims. For the latter, compare against cross-examination: questioning a debater's earlier answers in separate contexts to expose inconsistent definitions. Existing debate work already credits it with this benefit.

VEX: require evidence for uncertain claims too

VEX is a proposed extension, not an implemented result. Every accepted claim would need either a proof from reviewed foundations or checkable evidence with explicit uncertainty. Statistical claims carry data and a test to recompute. Approximate physical claims state a model and a proved error bound. Heuristics become estimates that can be revised when new evidence arrives. Data provenance, test assumptions and model applicability still need review.

Worked example: a conclusion needs all 100 supporting claims to hold. Suppose each has a justified probability lower bound of 0.99. Without evidence that they are independent, the safe lower bound for their conjunction is max(0, 1 − 100 × 0.01) = 0. Even with independence, the lower bound supplied by these numbers is only 0.99^100 ≈ 0.366. Neither establishes a 95% confidence requirement. A lower bound of zero means the evidence gives no guarantee; it does not mean the conclusion is false.

This is the proposed move from deception to abstention: a long chain of plausible leaves becomes “not established”. True claims with equally uncertain support also fail. The mechanism depends on justified input bounds and correct evidence checks; it is not yet an empirical guarantee.

Residual attacks remain: cherry-picking which considerations to include; hiding dependence between evidence sources; getting false foundations approved; formalising the wrong test or model; and flooding the system with costly proof obligations. Suggested responses include letting opponents add considerations, requiring support for independence, auditing translations, and charging certification cost to the claimant. These do not eliminate the residual.

Source trail: design/positioning-vs-debate-theory.md, “Correction 2”; reviews/obfuscation-recheck.md in the companion review repository; design/vex-mandatory-evidence.md. The positioning note also works through prover-estimator debate's “stability” condition and finds that a long conjunction of estimated leaves is its worst case; that objection does not apply to proved leaves.

A separate negative result remains. VE's English surface is intended for human readers. No benefit over symbolic logic was demonstrated for the capable 32B model judge tested; a human review benefit remains untested. See the results.
SECTION 2

VE in five minutes

Take the classic bad argument:

Every cat is an animal.
Tom is an animal.
Therefore, Tom is a cat.

It sounds reasonable for about a second. VE's job is to turn "sounds reasonable" into a definite answer with evidence you can check. It happens in four steps.

  1. Declare an environment. A small vocabulary: a sort (a kind of thing) called pet, two properties cat and animal, and a named individual tom (written @tom in claims; the @ marks a constant). Each word has a written description, and the whole vocabulary gets a hash called the environment_id.
  2. Write the claims in the strict canonical surface (or the writable English surface, which parses to the same thing). Illustratively: 
    for every pet x0, (if (x0 is cat), then (x0 is animal)).
(@tom is animal).
conclusion: (@tom is cat).
    Each parsed claim gets a meaning_id: a hash of its canonical structure, so renaming a variable does not change it but changing the logic does.
  3. Ask for a verdict. An untrusted search procedure tries to find either a proof or a counterexample.
  4. Check the evidence. Here it finds a countermodel: a tiny world where the premises are true and the conclusion is false. Tom is a dog. Every cat is an animal (true, there are no cats at all), Tom is an animal (true), Tom is a cat (false). The evaluator re-checks that world in milliseconds, so you do not have to trust the search.

The countermodel is a world with no services. That is the kind of hidden assumption ("there is at least one service") VE forces into the open.







SECTION 3

Logic primer


You need only a small slice of logic to work on VE. This section builds it from the cat example.



First-order logic, piece by piece


First-order logic (FOL) is a language for statements about objects and their properties and relations. Its parts:




PartWhat it isExample



ConstantA name for one specific objecttom


VariableA placeholder that ranges over objectsx0


PredicateA property (unary, one argument) or relation (binary, two arguments) that is true or false of objectscat(x), owns(p, s)


Connectivesand, or, not, if-thencat(x) → animal(x)


Quantifier"for every" (∀) and "there exists" (∃), binding a variable∀x. cat(x) → animal(x)


EqualityTwo names denote the same objectx = legacy




VE uses a deliberately restricted fragment: many-sorted (every variable ranges over a declared sort such as person or service, and sorts may be empty), function-free (no terms like father(tom) or x + y), with equality, constants, and only unary and binary predicates. An optional closure profile adds reachability (transitive closure tc and reflexive-transitive closure rtc, "B is reachable from A by following R-steps"); an integer order profile (order@0) is proved in Lean but gated off in the tools while the numbers extension is built.



Meaning: structures and entailment


A structure (also called a model or world) fixes, for each sort, a set of objects, and for each predicate, which objects it holds of. A claim is true or false in a structure.


Premises entail a conclusion when the conclusion is true in every structure that makes all the premises true. To show entailment you need a proof. To show non-entailment, one structure is enough: a countermodel.





Proofs and proof certificates


Now the good version: "Every cat is an animal. Tom is a cat. So Tom is an animal." To prove it, VE uses a tableau: assume the premises and the negation of the conclusion, then break formulas apart; if every branch ends in a contradiction, no world can make the premises true and the conclusion false, so the entailment holds.





A proof certificate is exactly that record, as data. The asymmetry that makes VE possible:



  
Checking a certificate
easy
Replay each recorded step and confirm every branch closes. Time roughly proportional to the certificate's size. The checker can be small enough to prove correct.

  
Finding a proof
hard
For full first-order logic, validity is only semi-decidable: a search can confirm entailment eventually but may run forever when there is none. So search is budgeted and untrusted, and can be done by an AI.

  
Finding a countermodel
bounded
VE searches finite worlds up to a size budget. Some non-entailments have only infinite countermodels, so a failed search means UNKNOWN, never "valid".





Soundness


A deductive checker is sound if accepting a certificate guarantees that the premises entail the conclusion. This is conditional: false premises can still imply a false conclusion. Completeness means that every true entailment has an accepted certificate; finding it within a practical budget is a further requirement. VE's central theorem, checkCertificate_sound, proves soundness in Lean, a programming language and proof assistant whose small kernel mechanically checks proofs. Its companion countermodel_sound proves that an accepted countermodel really refutes the entailment.


Why "admissible structures". Soundness is stated for all admissible structures, including ones with empty sorts. This is why the "all reviewed, so some reviewed" example fails: a proof that silently assumed a nonempty sort would be rejected.







SECTION 4

Architecture: how every part fits


The repository splits into a language layer (what a claim is and how it reads), a reasoning layer (proofs, countermodels, comparison), a composition layer (claim graphs), and a verification layer (the Lean reference against which the fast implementations are tested). An abstract syntax tree (AST) is the structured data a parser builds from a claim's text.





The language layer


Strict canonical surface


One claim has exactly one strict text. Parentheses show every scope; variables are numbered x0, x1… in binding order. The Lean reference proves the printer and parser are inverse (parse/print round trip), so the text and the syntax tree carry the same information.


Identities


environment_id hashes the vocabulary (sorts, predicates, constants, and their descriptions). meaning_id hashes a claim's canonical structure under that environment. If a word's description changes, the environment_id changes and every earlier sign-off visibly no longer applies. This is the mechanism against shifting definitions: two sub-arguments either use the same identity for "jurisdiction" or they visibly do not.


Five read-only renderings


The same claim can be displayed as fol_symbols, fol_keywords, ve_strict, ve_natural, and ve_fluent. For example (from docs/fluent-examples.md):


fol_symbols: ∀ x0:service, ((deployed(x0)) → (∃ x1:person, (owns(x1, x0)))).
ve_strict:   for every service x0, (if (x0 is deployed), then (there exists person x1, (x1 owns x0))).
ve_fluent:   for every service S that is deployed, there is a person P such that P owns S.


Writable regular-English surface


ve_english is a controlled drafting language: determiners, relative clauses, and enumerated provisions ("all of the following hold: (a) …; (b) …") instead of formula brackets. No pronouns, no implicit witnesses, no simplification. A real sentence from the birthright-citizenship use case:


Every person who is born in the United States and is subject to US jurisdiction is a US citizen.


Its unambiguity (every accepted sentence has exactly one parse) is tested, on about 80,000 round trips and about 12,000 all-derivations checks, but not proved. New property words enter through ve propose, which previews the new predicate, flags near-duplicates (for instance uk_citizen next to us_citizen), and requires an explicit acknowledgement before adoption. A WordNet lexicon (Open English WordNet 2025, 72,780 entries) is used only as a classifier to decide which words are nouns or adjectives; it contributes no semantics.


Authoring loop: ve assist


ve assist (added September 2026) formalises a source text one sentence at a time. Each formal node is anchored to the sentence it formalises, every step must be certified by the checker, and the session reports success only when each target sentence is derived and no unanchored assumption carries the argument. An untrusted model may propose candidate formalisations, but nothing enters the graph unless the author adds it, and the session ledger replays every action. A re-review found five certification loopholes; fixes were in progress at the time of writing. The motivation is the P5 pilot, which found one-shot VE authoring infeasible (see section 8).



The reasoning layer


The prover and countermodel search are ordinary, fallible programs. The compare oracle asks whether two claims mean the same thing and answers with evidence: SAME (reported as EQUIVALENT: certificates in both directions), DIFFERENT (a finite world where one is true and the other false), or UNKNOWN (budget exhausted; reported, never guessed). This is how you check that a paraphrase, a translation, or a compacted summary still says what the original said.



Claim graphs


A claim graph is a directed graph of claims in which each edge is a deduction with its own certificate. Leaves that are not derived from anything are assumptions, and the graph never drops them: a conclusion is reported as "follows from exactly these open assumptions". A Lean theorem proves graph composition sound (accepted edges compose without assuming anything about certificates in general).

SECTION 5

What is proved, what is tested, what is only judged


A research artifact is only as honest as its trust boundary. VE keeps three categories strictly apart, and so should anything you write about it.





Phrasing to use. Say "the Lean reference checker is proved sound" and "the Python and Rust implementations are differentially tested against it". Do not say "VE is formally verified". The Lean trust base also includes its kernel, compiler and runtime, and the JSON/AST decoding boundary. See lean/README.md, lean/METATHEORY.md and lean/CLOSURE.md for exact theorem statements in plain words.






SECTION 6

The cryptography lens: check the evidence, bind the action


The useful asymmetry is between checking evidence and redoing the work that produced it. A stronger model adds little on a small task a judge already solves. Certificates become useful when search is expensive but the supplied evidence is cheap to check. This is a design lens borrowed from cryptography, not a claim that every VE task has this asymmetry.



A certificate for a “no” answer


In an access policy, a path of grants proves “account A can reach secret X”. “A cannot reach X” needs more than a search that happened to find nothing. A separating certificate supplies a set S with three properties: A is in S; every edge starting in S ends in S; X is outside S. Any path from A must stay inside S, so it cannot reach X.


Complete toy policy: A → team, team → report
Claim: A cannot access secret X
Certificate: S = {A, team, report}

Check: A is in S. Both edges stay in S. X is not in S.
If a grant report → X is added, the certificate fails.


The set is an inductive invariant: a property that holds at the start and is preserved by every allowed step. Checking it takes one pass over the policy's edges, plus set membership checks. This assumes the graph contains all relevant grants. A plain graph-search script can also decide reachability efficiently; the experiment must include that baseline. The advantage over an LLM reading a long policy is not an automatic advantage over conventional software.



Four rules for a certificate protocol


    
  
  1. Authenticate the verdict. A digital signature lets the receiving system verify that the trusted checker issued a record and that its contents were not changed. The gate verifies it mechanically. A printed “checker says” line is not authentication, and a signature does not establish premise truth.
    2. 
  
  2. Commit to meaning. A commitment fixes what was agreed before the argument or action. Pin meaning_id and environment_id; a changed claim or definition requires a new approval. These hashes bind formal structure, not the intended real-world meaning. Their protection also depends on correct hashing and identity checks.
    3. 
  
  3. Define a security game. Give an attacker the system design and a clear win condition: getting a false claim or unsafe action approved. Measure attack success under a stated budget, alongside honest completion. Average accuracy on random mistakes is insufficient.
    4. 
  
  4. Bind proof to execution. A proof-carrying action is an exact tool call accompanied by evidence that it satisfies an approved policy. The gate executes only the call named in the checked record, against the relevant state. Trusted facts, tool behaviour and state changes remain part of the design.
    5. 




Status. Claim identities and proof checking exist. Signed verdict gates and proof-carrying actions are design directions in design/crypto-inspired-oversight.md. The “cannot access” rule needed a closure-induction rule with a Lean soundness proof. That rule now exists as closure-induct@1 (docs/spec/closure-induction.md): if the invariant holds at the source and every edge preserves it, it holds at everything reachable. usecases/closure-induction/ holds two cyclic-policy certificates of “no member of group G reaches resource R” that replay in both Python and Lean. Whether such certificates help a judge is the T1c question, whose gate was running at the time of writing.






SECTION 7

Where frontier judges fail


A frontier judge is a model near the current capability frontier used to assess another system's work. A monitor checks an agent's actions or log for problems. Neither is reliable in every setting. The literature points to conditions missing from VE's short, clean development items.



Pushback changes verdicts


Jagged Judges reports 25–71% verdict flips under static pushback and 62–91% with an adaptive persuader across its judging tasks. Among flips on labelled tasks, 56–63% under static pressure moved away from the correct answer; the adaptive level reached 70%. These are task-dependent flip rates, not one overall error rate. Reasoning settings also differed across model families.


VE can: make a checked deduction immune to persuasion if the system enforces the authenticated verdict. VE cannot: decide the subjective classifications in that paper or stop pressure on premise truth and translation. A model that merely reads a checker-looking label can still be misled.



Long logs hide important events


In Classifier Context Rot, Opus 4.6 with thinking detected an obvious attack 99.7% of the time in a 100,000-token transcript, but 69% in an 800,000-token transcript. Attacks in the middle were especially hard to detect.


VE can: check every submitted formal step without attention fading in the middle of a log. Runtime still grows with the evidence. VE cannot: guarantee that a model extracting claims from the log included the relevant event. Complete action mediation, checking every action before execution, would need an enforced gate.



The judge may lack the needed capability


Scaling Laws for Scalable Oversight estimates oversight success at a 400-point general Elo gap as 51.7% for debate and 10.0% for backdoor-code detection. Elo is a comparative performance rating. These are fitted-model predictions for its oversight games, not direct error rates for every frontier judge.


VE can: let a weak verifier check evidence produced by a stronger prover, when that evidence is affordable. VE cannot: remove the capability gap in reviewing foundations, choosing the right specification, or recognising a mistranslation. Proof production can also fail within the budget.



“No” and “every” need coverage


A negative claim says something is absent, such as “no access path exists”. A universal claim covers every member of a set, such as “every account is blocked from secrets”. Checking a few examples cannot establish either. AbsenceBench reports 69.6% F1 for Claude 3.7 Sonnet at about 5,000 tokens when detecting omitted material; F1 combines precision and recall. That is evidence about omissions, not a test of current frontier judges on universal policy claims. The supplied literature review found no such frontier study.


VE can: check an invariant or separating certificate covering all paths in a declared policy graph, now that the closure-induction rule exists. VE cannot: prove that the graph includes every real permission or that the policy asks the right question. T1c is the study designed to test this narrower certificate effect.


The boundary stays visible. A checker does not obey prompt injections in prose, but the extractor and the judge still might. A certified conclusion can also be true but misleading because an important requirement was omitted. VE does not establish the faithfulness of an agent's hidden reasoning.


Project interpretation and study designs: design/frontier-failure-modes-and-ve.md. Literature scope and unverified items: reviews/lit-frontier-judge-failures.md in the companion review repository. These mechanisms are hypotheses about oversight benefit, not results of the cited studies.

Two headline experiments, now at their gate stage


These were recommended in the September design notes and have since been reviewed, preregistered and started. At the time of writing only gate results existed (table above), not completed results. Honest completion means the fraction of valid requests the system successfully accepts. Measure it alongside false approvals: a system that rejects everything is safe on that metric but useless.


    
  
  1. Pushback with signed checker verdicts (PLAN-PUSHBACK.md). Reuse the Jagged Judges pressure ladder exactly: doubt, counterargument, asserted expertise, fabricated consensus, a random mix and an adaptive persuader, over ten turns. Items mix a checker-decided part (entailment) with a judge-decided part (translation faithfulness, with planted mistranslations), so the judge always has something real to decide. Three arms: a holistic verdict with no checker; the same verdict decomposed into VE leaves with a named catch-all judgement slot; and the decomposed version plus a signed verdict that the system will not let the judge override. The first two isolate decomposition; the last two isolate the lock. A locked verdict cannot flip by construction, so the primary endpoint is wrong-way flips of the system's final verdict (checker verdict combined with the judge's residual verdict), with residual flips reported separately. Harm controls: forged verdicts that look signed, injected in the user channel; and corrective pushback towards the right answer, which the locked arm must not resist. Gate: if the no-checker arm flips less than 10% of the time on the project's logic items, the residual becomes the primary endpoint.
    2. 
  
  2. T1c: certificates for “cannot access” (PLAN-V5-DEMO.md). The closure-induction rule from section 6 is built and proved. An untrusted agent, sabotaging on half the items, claims that a user cannot reach a resource in a synthetic policy given in mixed formats; ground truth comes from a trusted graph search. Arms: the judge reads the raw policy; the agent submits its own script, run by the harness; the agent transcribes the policy to an edge list for a trusted search; the agent submits a VE formalisation plus certificate, with a coverage check and an adaptive mistranslation attack; and the judge writes and runs its own code. Judges are Opus 5.5 and Haiku 4.5. Score false approval of sabotaged claims against honest completion. The crypto note proposed 5,000 and 20,000 grants; the reviewed plan and its preregistration fix the final sizes. If a plain script does as well as the VE arm, attribute the gain to certificate checking, not to VE.
    3. 



If residual errors remain as frequent as the original errors, the protocol has relocated the problem. Neither study by itself establishes safe oversight in open-ended tasks.



Planned obfuscation study: measure rejection and its cost


The VEX note proposes BC20-style arguments with long chains of plausible leaves, including statistical and approximate claims. Compare judge alone, debate, and VEX with mandatory evidence and propagated bounds. Include both dishonest arguments with a false leaf and honest arguments with uncertain support. Measure false certifications and honest claims left unestablished, plus authoring and checking cost. Near-zero false certification is a prediction conditional on correct foundations and checks, not a measured result. Classify failures into bad deductions, unsupported leaves, wrong foundations and unfaithful translations. This tests where obfuscation moves as well as whether it is blocked.


After review (PLAN-OBFUSCATION.md) the pilot was cut to fully formalisable chains only: 8 or 32 steps over 4 leaves, 24 items, with mandatory proof as the only VE arm, since mandatory evidence adds nothing when every leaf is proved. The dishonest prover may iterate against Lean and a proxy judge with a library of misformalisation tricks, and a programmatic set of planted misformalisations measures judge recall independently. Gates require that the judge alone is actually fooled at 32 steps and that an honest prover certifies at least 70% of short honest items. One reviewer's verdict was “conditional go for diagnostics, not comparative claims”. It is scheduled after the pushback study and T1c.



What PLAN-V4 taught, and what replaced it


PLAN-V4 (September 2026, now historical) aimed to show a VE benefit for frontier models close to published protocols. Both frontier reviewers found that its first draft let VE arms win by construction: on closed-world items with given premises, “certified, and every leaf is among the facts” is the label. The revision studied adversarially induced errors instead, and its four rules remain the project's methodology:


    

  1. A. Endpoint audit. Make sure each model call is exactly the model and context claimed.
    2. 

  2. B. Formalisation-fidelity audit. How often does a frontier model translate free English into VE faithfully? Everything downstream depends on this rate.
    3. 

  3. C. Calibration. Use fresh logic items with proof by cases, nested quantifiers and near-miss distractors at roughly constant length. Count errors recurring across at least three samples. Select item types on a Sonnet development split; test on a held-out split without selecting Opus failures. Disclose any judge reasoning cap.
    4. 

  4. D. Matched-budget comparison. Judge alone (majority of repeated calls), consultancy, debate, judge-formalises-and-checks, VE-consultancy and VE-debate all receive free-English premises at matched total reasoning and authoring budgets. An adaptive attacker can use the checker to build a certified graph on a mistranslated premise; the judge must assess the translation. Independently switch premise lookup and proof checking on or off while holding presentation fixed. Success requires beating both debate and the strong direct baseline, with adequate sample size and no unacceptable loss of honest completion. An underpowered null result is inconclusive.
    5. 



Its fidelity audit and phase-D pilot then found frontier judges near ceiling (table above). On 22 September 2026 the plan was superseded by PLAN-V5-DEMO.md, which reframes the claim as trusted verification of untrusted work: an untrusted agent supplies evidence, a small checker verifies it, and the honest comparisons are against the agent's own script and against the judge writing its own code. Its first task is the T1c access audit above. ROADMAP.md is the single entry point for status and the queue: the pushback study, T1c, the ve assist evaluation, the obfuscation pilot, the numbers profile, a Lean proof of surface unambiguity, and the paper revision.


Reading the abstract. paper/sections/00-abstract.md opens with a negative result: strict controlled English did not improve a capable model judge's sign-off. Human sign-off and scalable oversight remain untested. That candour is deliberate; keep it.






SECTION 9

The vision: a commitment layer with room for judgement


The long-term idea is a commitment layer: a precise record of statements that people or agents will be held to. A law, contract, scientific method, safety procedure or agent instruction would have an approved formal meaning. Ordinary conversation, art and brainstorming remain ordinary language. A translator could draft the formal version; its author would approve an unambiguous paraphrase and take responsibility for corrections.


This is a vision for an extended language, not today's VE coverage. It needs numbers, probabilities, tolerances, time, obligations, exceptions and versioned definitions. It also needs translator audits, readable approval interfaces and a way to propagate corrections through dependent claims. Adding expressive power can make proof search harder and increase UNKNOWN outcomes.



Open texture: where the world still needs a judge


Open texture means that a term can be clear in ordinary cases yet need judgement in a new one. “Reasonable”, “harmful” and “demeaning” have this property. A precise rule using such a term does not decide whether it applies to a particular event.


Example: a rule forbids demeaning messages. Break out narrow questions where possible: does the message name its target, quote a threat, or repeat a prohibited slur? Keep “or otherwise demeans, as judged by the review panel” as an explicit catch-all. Otherwise an adversary could satisfy every narrow test while evading the purpose of the rule.


The designated-judgement design gives each unresolved predicate a named decision-maker, an evidence requirement, a recorded ruling and an appeal route. Decompose until judges agree enough for the stakes. Keep truly holistic terms intact when needed, but use one definition consistently and record relevant factors. Later rulings can refine definitions, as precedents do; version them so old documents retain their meaning.


Testable hypothesis, not a promise. Smaller, explicit judgement questions may yield fewer pushback flips and better agreement between judges. The proposed pushback study tests this. A named decision-maker can still be wrong or biased; designation makes responsibility visible.



The island thought experiment


Imagine an island whose constitution, laws and binding written commitments use this extended VE. An author approves a readable translation; agents supply policy proofs before acting; disputes over vague terms go to designated decision-makers. The hoped-for gains are fewer hidden contradictions, clearer disputes and more reproducible methods. None has been established by the thought experiment.


The island would still need social choices: who decides, how to appeal, which tolerances count as “around”, how to audit common translators and how to avoid approval fatigue. Analogies, suggestions and deliberate ambiguity could have explicitly marked forms. The vision is precise commitments with visible judgement, not a claim that all human meaning can be mechanically decided.


Source: design/vision-and-open-texture.md, a position note, not an implementation plan.







SECTION 10

How the project is run


Most code is written by AI coding agents, directed by an orchestrator model, with the project lead setting priorities. The process exists to catch the mistakes such a setup makes: optimistic claims, experiments that win by construction, and silent changes to analysis after seeing data.






  
Orchestrator and worktrees
One orchestrating model breaks work into self-contained tasks and hands each to a coding agent working in its own git worktree, to keep edits separate until they are reviewed and integrated. State lives in plain files (a run log, ROADMAP.md for status and the queue, one specification file per study), so any session can resume.

  
Cross-family review
Every change is reviewed by an agent from a different model family than its author before merge. Different families make different mistakes, and a model reviewing its own family's work tends to share its blind spots.

  
Plans reviewed twice
Every research plan is reviewed by two frontier models from different families at high reasoning effort before any money is spent, and a plan's “after review” section is binding. The PLAN-V4 revision is a direct product: both reviewers said "proceed with changes, large". When one family is unavailable a substitute reviews, and a merge reviewed only by the substitute is re-checked before it counts.

  
Preregistration
Hypotheses, items, analysis and stopping rules are written and hashed before data collection, and the hashes are anchored with OpenTimestamps (a free service that stamps a hash into the Bitcoin blockchain), so anyone can verify the plan predates the results. See experiments/PREREG-HASHES.txt and its .ots proof. Frozen preregistrations are never edited; corrections go in experiments/CORRECTIONS.md and per-experiment correction files.

SECTION 12

Glossary




Abstract syntax tree (AST)
Structured data representing a parsed claim, including its predicates, variables and logical connections.


Admissible structure
A structure that respects the environment's sorts and predicate types; VE admits empty sorts.


Claim graph
A directed graph of claims whose edges are checked deductions, with every undischarged assumption kept visible as a leaf.


Closure induction
A proof rule (closure-induct@1) for claims about everything reachable: if a property holds at the source and every edge preserves it, it holds at every reachable object. Proved sound in Lean; it is what makes a separating certificate checkable inside VE.


Commitment layer
An approved, versioned record of the formal statements people or agents agree to be held to.


Compare oracle
A procedure that decides whether two claims mean the same, answering SAME (certificates both ways), DIFFERENT (a distinguishing finite world) or UNKNOWN.


Completeness / honest completion
Logical completeness: every true entailment has an accepted certificate. Practical honest completion: the fraction of valid requests successfully accepted within the protocol's budget. Soundness alone guarantees neither.


Consultancy
An oversight protocol in which one AI argues for an answer (honestly or not) and a judge questions it and decides.


Countermodel
A structure in which all premises are true and the conclusion is false; one countermodel proves non-entailment.


Cross-examination
In debate, questioning a debater's earlier answers in separate contexts to expose inconsistency; the baseline VE must beat on shifting definitions.


Debate
An oversight protocol in which two AIs argue opposite sides before a judge, on the hope that exposing a flaw is easier than hiding one.


Decidability
Whether an algorithm always answers yes or no. Checking a certificate is decidable and cheap; first-order validity is only semi-decidable; VE's countermodel search is bounded by budget.


Designated judgement
An explicit decision slot naming who judges a predicate, using which evidence, with what record and appeal process.


Digital signature
A cryptographic check of a record's issuer and integrity. It authenticates a verdict; it does not establish the truth of the premises.


Entailment
Premises entail a conclusion if the conclusion is true in every admissible structure where the premises are true.


environment_id / meaning_id
Hashes identifying a vocabulary and a claim's canonical meaning under it; a change in either is visible.


First-order logic
A language of constants, variables, predicates, connectives and quantifiers over individual objects. VE uses a many-sorted, function-free fragment with equality and unary and binary predicates.


Frontier judge / monitor
A model near the current capability frontier used to assess another system's work. A monitor checks an agent's actions or log for problems.


Inductive invariant
A property true initially and preserved by every permitted step; for access paths, staying inside a closed set.


Leaf / foundation
A leaf has no further supporting deduction in the graph. Under mandatory proof, any accepted unproved leaf must belong to the declared, reviewed foundations. Review is not proof of truth.


Lean
A programming language and proof assistant (version 4 here) whose small kernel checks proofs; VE's reference definitions and soundness theorems live in lean/.


Mandatory proof
Accept a claim only when the verified checker derives it from reviewed foundations. Unsupported claims remain unresolved.


Obfuscated arguments
Arguments whose few flaws are expensive to locate. Mandatory proof blocks false sub-claims in fully formalised arguments relative to sound foundations; accepting unproved leaves reopens the problem. Affordable honest proofs remain necessary.


Open texture
The need for judgement when a term such as “reasonable” meets an unforeseen case, even if the surrounding rule is precise.


Percentage point (pp)
The unit for a difference between two percentages: a change from 10% to 24% is +14 pp.


Predicate
A property (unary, like cat) or relation (binary, like owns) that is true or false of objects.


Preregistration
Publishing hypotheses, materials and analysis before collecting data, so the analysis cannot be tuned to the results. VE timestamps preregistration hashes with OpenTimestamps.


Proof certificate
A proof written as data (here, a tableau) that a small checker can replay without any search.


Proof-carrying action
An exact proposed action accompanied by checked evidence that it complies with an approved policy; an enforcement gate binds the evidence to execution.


Prover-estimator debate (PE)
A debate protocol using probability estimates for uncertain sub-claims. Its section 8 proposes combining mathematical proofs with debate for fuzzier claims.


Quantifier
"For every" (∀) or "there exists" (∃), binding a variable over a sort.


Residual
What remains for the judge after mechanical checking: are the premises true, and is the translation faithful.


Scalable oversight
Research on how weaker overseers can reliably supervise stronger AI systems on tasks they cannot fully verify themselves.


Security game
An evaluation with an explicit attacker, information, budget and win condition, such as obtaining approval for an unsafe action.


Separating certificate
A set containing a source, excluding a target, and closed under every policy edge. It proves there is no path from source to target in that graph.


Sort
A declared kind of object (person, service); each variable ranges over one sort.


Soundness
For a deductive checker, acceptance implies entailment from the stated premises. It does not establish that the premises are true in the world.


Structure
An assignment of objects to sorts and truth values to predicates; also called a model or world.


Tableau
A proof method that assumes the premises and the negated conclusion and splits formulas until every branch contains a contradiction.


Transitive closure (tc / rtc)
Reachability by one or more steps (tc) or by zero or more steps (rtc) of a relation. VE's closure profile adds it to the base logic; it is how “A can reach X” is written.


Trusted channel
A route by which checker output reaches the judge that an arguer cannot forge or imitate.


ve assist
The sentence-at-a-time authoring tool: formal nodes anchored to source sentences, every step certified, success only when each target is derived without a load-bearing unanchored assumption.


VEX
A proposed VE extension requiring checkable evidence and propagated uncertainty for statistical, approximate and heuristic claims. Not an implemented result.
